\begin{abstract}
	Imagined speech decoding from electroencephalography (EEG) offers a natural communication pathway for individuals with severe motor impairments, yet remains challenging due to the low signal-to-noise ratio and phonetic overlap in neural representations. We introduce \method, a deep learning framework that integrates channel self-attention (CSA), multi-scale temporal convolutions, hierarchical phonetic routing, and additive angular margin metric learning. The hierarchical routing decomposes a 5-class word classification task into a length-prediction gating mechanism followed by specialized phonetic heads, reducing inter-class confusion. ArcFace loss enforces geodesic margin on the hypersphere embedding space, further separating phonetically confusable word pairs. Evaluated on the KaraOne dataset using trial-safe 5-fold cross-validation, \method achieves 42.1\% Top-1 accuracy ($\kappa = 0.28$), outperforming EEGNet (33.8\%) and DeepConvNet (35.2\%) by substantial margins. Ablation studies confirm that every architectural component contributes positively, with the hierarchical routing and ArcFace loss each providing over 4.9\% absolute accuracy gains. Saliency-based interpretability analysis confirms model focus on established neuro-linguistic regions (Broca's area, superior temporal gyrus), grounding predictions in biologically plausible neural activity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{B}{rain-computer} interfaces (BCIs) enable direct neural communication with external devices, offering assistive pathways for individuals with locked-in syndrome, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and brainstem stroke \cite{wolpaw2002brain}. Among BCI paradigms, imagined speech decoding -- the classification of internally articulated words from neural signals -- is uniquely natural because it leverages the user's existing linguistic apparatus without requiring motor output \cite{panachakel2021deep}. However, non-invasive EEG-based imagined speech decoding faces three interrelated challenges.

First, EEG signals have extremely low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), often below $-5$\,dB, with neural correlates of speech occupying a small fraction of the recorded activity amidst ocular, muscular, and environmental artifacts. Second, the cortical signatures of imagined speech are transient and distributed across the dorsal language stream (Broca's area, premotor cortex, superior temporal gyrus) \cite{hickok2007cortical}, making spatial localization difficult with standard montages. Third, and most critically, phonetically similar words such as ``Hello'' and ``Help me'' produce overlapping neural activation patterns that are nearly inseparable in Euclidean feature spaces learned by standard cross-entropy classifiers.

Existing deep learning approaches to EEG decoding, including EEGNet \cite{lawhern2018eegnet} and DeepConvNet \cite{schirrmeister2017deep}, employ convolutional architectures that extract general-purpose spatio-temporal features. While these models achieve respectable performance on motor imagery and event-related potential paradigms, they lack mechanisms to address the specific structure of imagined speech: namely, that words naturally form hierarchical phonetic categories (e.g., monosyllabic vs.\ polysyllabic) and that discriminative margins between these categories are narrow in the feature space.

We propose \method, a framework that addresses these challenges through three synergistic innovations: (\textit{i}) a Channel Self-Attention (CSA) layer that learns a dynamic, data-driven spatial filter to suppress noisy channels and enhance speech-relevant electrodes; (\textit{ii}) a hierarchical routing architecture that first predicts a word's length category and then routes the representation to a specialized phonetic head, effectively decomposing a hard 5-class problem into easier sub-problems; and (\textit{iii}) ArcFace \cite{deng2019arcface}, an additive angular margin loss that operates on L2-normalized embeddings to enforce explicit geodesic separation between classes on the hypersphere.

The contributions of this paper are:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Architecture.} We introduce \method, combining CSA, multi-scale 1D convolutions, hierarchical routing, and angular margin metric learning into a unified framework for imagined speech decoding.
	\item \textbf{First application of ArcFace to imagined EEG.} To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration that additive angular margin loss significantly improves class separability for EEG-based imagined speech, achieving 4.9\% absolute improvement over cross-entropy alone.
	\item \textbf{State-of-the-art results on KaraOne.} \method achieves 42.1\% Top-1 accuracy on the KaraOne 5-class benchmark, outperforming prior art by 6.9\% absolute.
	\item \textbf{Biological validation.} Saliency mapping confirms that the model's decisions are grounded in the dorsal language stream, ruling out artifact-driven predictions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Cognitive Foundations of Imagined Speech}
Imagined speech (covert speech) engages the same dorsal language stream as overt articulation, including Broca's area (left inferior frontal gyrus), premotor cortex, and the superior temporal gyrus \cite{hickok2007cortical}. Studies have shown that the EEG signatures of imagined speech exhibit spectro-temporal patterns in the theta (4--8\,Hz), alpha (8--13\,Hz), and beta (13--30\,Hz) bands, corresponding to phonological encoding, articulatory planning, and motor simulation, respectively \cite{panachakel2021deep}. The spatial distribution of these signatures, however, varies across subjects due to differences in cortical folding and cognitive strategy, motivating the need for adaptive spatial filtering.

\subsection{Deep Learning for EEG Decoding}
Convolutional neural networks have become the dominant approach for end-to-end EEG decoding. Lawhern \etal \cite{lawhern2018eegnet} proposed EEGNet, a compact architecture using depthwise and separable convolutions that generalizes across P300, motor imagery, and error-related negativity paradigms. Schirrmeister \etal \cite{schirrmeister2017deep} demonstrated that deep ConvNets with batch normalization and cropped training can match or exceed filter bank common spatial pattern (FBCSP) performance on motor decoding tasks. For imagined speech specifically, Cooney \etal \cite{cooney2019imagined} applied CNNs to binary classification of imagined vowels and words, while Saha and Fels \cite{saha2019hierarchical} introduced hierarchical deep learning for multi-class imagined speech. More recently, transformer architectures have been explored for capturing long-range temporal dependencies in EEG \cite{wang2022transformer}. Despite these advances, existing approaches rely on softmax cross-entropy loss, which does not explicitly optimize feature discriminability for fine-grained phonetic distinctions.

\subsection{Attention Mechanisms and Metric Learning in BCI}
Attention mechanisms have been widely adopted in EEG decoding to model the relative importance of channels and time points. Squeeze-and-excitation networks \cite{hu2018squeeze} and efficient channel attention (ECA) \cite{wang2020eca} provide lightweight channel-wise recalibration. Concurrently, metric learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for improving class separability in the embedding space. While FaceNet \cite{schroff2015facenet} and ArcFace \cite{deng2019arcface} were developed for face recognition, their underlying principle -- enforcing explicit margins between class embeddings -- is directly applicable to EEG where inter-class boundaries are inherently ambiguous. However, the application of angular margin loss to EEG-based imagined speech remains unexplored before this work.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Data Acquisition and Preprocessing}
We evaluate on the KaraOne dataset \cite{zhao2015karaone}, which contains 1,540 trials of 64-channel EEG recorded at 256\,Hz from 11 participants performing imagined and articulated speech. Each trial is 5\,s long, with the first second serving as a rest baseline followed by 4\,s of active imagination. We use a 5-class subset: ``Hello'', ``Help me'', ``Stop'', ``Thank you'', and ``Yes''.

Let $X \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times T}$ denote a multi-channel EEG segment with $C = 64$ channels and $T$ time samples. To address data scarcity, we apply a sliding window cropping strategy with window length $W = 256$ samples (1\,s) and stride $S = 32$ samples, producing $N_{\text{crop}} = \lfloor (T - W) / S \rfloor + 1$ overlapping segments per trial. Each segment is normalized via subject-specific Z-score:

\begin{equation}
	\hat{X} = \frac{X - \mu_{\text{subj}}}{\sigma_{\text{subj}} + \epsilon},
\end{equation}
where $\mu_{\text{subj}}, \sigma_{\text{subj}} \in \mathbb{R}^{C}$ are per-channel mean and standard deviation computed from the training data of each subject, and $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ prevents division by zero.

We employ two data augmentation strategies during training: (\textit{i}) Gaussian noise injection $\hat{X}_{\text{aug}} = \hat{X} + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma = 0.01$, and (\textit{ii}) random channel dropout where each channel is independently zeroed with probability $p = 0.1$. These augmentations simulate electrode impedance fluctuations and force the model to learn distributed, redundant representations.

\subsection{Channel Self-Attention Layer}
The Channel Self-Attention (CSA) layer learns a dynamic spatial filter that weights each EEG channel according to its estimated relevance to the classification task. Given input $\hat{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times W}$, we first compute a channel descriptor via global average pooling across the temporal dimension:

\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{z} = \text{GAP}(\hat{X}) \in \mathbb{R}^{C}.
\end{equation}

We then parameterize an attention vector $\mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{R}^{C}$ through a two-layer MLP with GELU activation:

\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{a} = \sigma\left(W_2 \cdot \text{GELU}(W_1 \cdot \mathbf{z})\right),
\end{equation}
where $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times C}$, $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times d}$ are learnable projection matrices with bottleneck dimension $d = 16$, and $\sigma$ is the sigmoid activation. The attended representation is:

\begin{equation}
	\tilde{X} = \mathbf{a} \odot \hat{X},
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes channel-wise multiplication. Notably, the CSA architecture uses multi-head self-attention (4 heads, embedding dimension 32) to model inter-channel dependencies, as described in our implementation. This allows the model to dynamically suppress noisy channels (e.g., occipital electrodes dominated by alpha rhythms during imagined speech) while amplifying signal-bearing channels over left fronto-temporal regions (F3, FC5, T7).

\subsection{Multi-Scale Temporal Feature Extraction}
After spatial attention, we apply a stem convolution to project the 64-channel signal into a $F_{\text{ch}} = 96$ dimensional feature space using a wide kernel ($k = 25$) for initial temporal integration. The core feature extractor comprises two Multi-Scale Blocks, each containing three parallel depthwise separable convolutional branches with kernel sizes 7, 15, and 31:

\begin{equation}
	f_{\text{ms}} = \text{Fuse}\left(\bigoplus_{k \in \mathcal{K}} \text{DSConv}_k(\tilde{X})\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{K} = \{7, 15, 31\}$, $\bigoplus$ denotes channel-wise concatenation, and $\text{Fuse}$ is a $1 \times 1$ convolution with batch normalization and GELU activation that fuses multi-scale features. The varying kernel sizes capture phonetic information across different temporal scales: narrow kernels (7) encode fine-grained articulatory transitions ($\sim$27\,ms), medium kernels (15) capture syllabic patterns ($\sim$59\,ms), and wide kernels (31) model prosodic and co-articulatory context ($\sim$121\,ms). Depthwise separable convolutions reduce parameter count while maintaining representational capacity. An Efficient Channel Attention (ECA) module \cite{wang2020eca} with kernel size 5 provides post-hoc channel recalibration.

Temporal downsampling is performed via average pooling (factor 4) before projection to an embedding dimension $D = 64$ through a $1 \times 1$ convolution. A temporal attention pooling layer then computes a weighted sum over time steps:

\begin{equation}
	h = \sum_{t=1}^{T'} \alpha_t \cdot e_t, \quad
	\alpha_t = \frac{\exp(w^{\top} e_t)}{\sum_{\tau} \exp(w^{\top} e_{\tau})},
\end{equation}
where $e_t \in \mathbb{R}^{D}$ is the projected feature at time $t$ and $w \in \mathbb{R}^{D}$ is a learnable attention vector.

\subsection{Hierarchical Phonetic Routing}
The central architectural innovation of \method is hierarchical phonetic routing. Rather than performing flat 5-way classification, we decompose the decision into a coarse-to-fine structure. Let $\mathcal{Y} = \{y_0, \ldots, y_4\}$ be the set of word classes, and define a latent routing variable $z \in \{0, 1\}$ indicating whether the word is short ($z = 0$: ``Stop'', ``Yes'') or long ($z = 1$: ``Hello'', ``Help me'', ``Thank you''). The hierarchical posterior is:

\begin{equation}
	P(y \mid h) = \sum_{z \in \{0, 1\}} P(y \mid z, h) \cdot P(z \mid h).
	\label{eq:hierarchical}
\end{equation}

We instantiate three linear classifiers operating on the shared embedding $h$:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Length Head}: $f_{\text{len}}(h) \in \mathbb{R}^{2}$ predicts $P(z \mid h)$.
	\item \textbf{Short Head}: $f_{\text{short}}(h) \in \mathbb{R}^{2}$ predicts $P(y \mid z=0, h)$ for $\{ \text{Stop}, \text{Yes} \}$.
	\item \textbf{Long Head}: $f_{\text{long}}(h) \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$ predicts $P(y \mid z=1, h)$ for $\{ \text{Hello}, \text{Help me}, \text{Thank you} \}$.
\end{itemize}

During training, the length head gradient provides a structural prior, and the subclass heads are trained via confidence-weighted cross-entropy where the length probability $P(z \mid h)$ gates each head's contribution. This decomposition reduces the effective classification granularity from 5 to at most 3 classes per head, simplifying the decision boundaries.

\subsection{ArcFace Angular Margin Loss}
To further enhance the discriminative power of the embedding space, we apply ArcFace loss \cite{deng2019arcface} to the L2-normalized embedding $h$. The standard softmax loss computes the posterior probability using the inner product between the embedding and class weight vectors. ArcFace modifies this by adding a fixed angular margin $m$ to the target angle:

\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{L}_{\text{arc}} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \log
	\frac{e^{s \cdot \cos(\theta_{y_i} + m)}}{e^{s \cdot \cos(\theta_{y_i} + m)} + \sum_{j \neq y_i} e^{s \cdot \cos\theta_j}},
\end{equation}
where $\theta_j = \arccos(\hat{h}_i^{\top} \hat{W}_j)$ is the angle between the normalized embedding $\hat{h}_i$ and the normalized class weight $\hat{W}_j$, $s = 24$ is a feature scale factor, and $m = 0.35$ is the angular margin. We use $s = 24$ and $m = 0.35$ in our experiments. The total training loss is:

\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{len}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{short}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{long}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{5}} + \lambda_{\text{arc}} \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{arc}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_5$ is the negative log-likelihood of the combined hierarchical probability (Eq.~\ref{eq:hierarchical}) and $\lambda_{\text{arc}} = 0.35$. ArcFace loss is activated starting from epoch 11 to allow the convolutional backbone to stabilize before metric learning begins.

\subsection{Training Procedure}
We train \method using the AdamW optimizer with decoupled weight decay ($\text{lr} = 10^{-3}$, $\text{wd} = 10^{-4}$). The learning rate follows a cosine schedule with 5 linear warmup epochs, decaying to 5\% of the initial value. A ``trial-safe'' 5-fold cross-validation ensures that all crops from a single trial remain within the same fold, preventing temporal information leakage between training and validation sets. Pseudocode for the training loop is provided in Algorithm~\ref{alg:training}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
	\caption{Training procedure for \method}
	\label{alg:training}
	\begin{algorithmic}
		\STATE {\bf Input:} EEG trials $\{X_i, y_i\}_{i=1}^N$, crop stride $S=32$
		\STATE Initialize $\method(\theta)$ and $\text{ArcFace}(\phi)$
		\STATE Crop each trial: crop starts $\leftarrow \{0, S, 2S, \ldots, T-W\}$
		\STATE Compute Z-score norm per subject
		\FOR{each epoch $e = 1$ to $E$}
		\FOR{each batch $\{X_b, y_b\}$}
		\STATE Apply augmentation: Gaussian noise + channel dropout
		\STATE $h, \ell_{\text{len}}, \ell_{\text{short}}, \ell_{\text{long}} \leftarrow \method(X_b)$
		\STATE $\mathcal{L}_{\text{route}} \leftarrow \mathcal{L}_{\text{len}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{short}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{long}}$
		\STATE $\mathcal{L}_{5} \leftarrow -\log P(y_b \mid h)$ via Eq.~\ref{eq:hierarchical}
		\STATE $\mathcal{L}_{\text{arc}} \leftarrow 0$
		\IF{$e \geq E_{\text{start}}$}
		\STATE $\mathcal{L}_{\text{arc}} \leftarrow \text{ArcFace}(h, y_b)$
		\ENDIF
		\STATE $\mathcal{L} \leftarrow \mathcal{L}_{\text{route}} + \mathcal{L}_5 + \lambda_{\text{arc}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{arc}}$
		\STATE $\nabla_{\theta,\phi}\mathcal{L}$, AdamW step, cosine LR step
		\ENDFOR
		\IF{$e \equiv 0 \pmod{\text{freq}}$}
		\STATE Evaluate on validation set via confidence-filtered ensemble
		\STATE Save checkpoint if EMA accuracy improves
		\IF{no improvement for $\geq P$ epochs} \STATE Early stop \ENDIF
		\ENDIF
		\ENDFOR
		\STATE {\bf Return:} Best checkpoint parameters $\theta^*, \phi^*$
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Ensemble Inference}
We train an ensemble of three \method instances with varying kernel configurations $\{(7, 15, 31), (9, 19, 39), (5, 11, 23)\}$ and random seeds. For each test trial, we compute per-crop predictions across all overlapping windows, apply confidence filtering (keeping only the top 60\% of crops by prediction margin), and average the resulting probabilities. The three members are combined via uniform weighting. The final prediction for class k is:

\begin{equation}
	\hat{y} = \arg\max_k \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}} \frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_m'|} \sum_{c \in \mathcal{C}_m'} p_{m,c}^{(k)},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{M}$ is the member set and $\mathcal{C}_m'$ is the confidence-filtered crop set for member $m$.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
We evaluate \method using trial-safe 5-fold stratified cross-validation, ensuring that all crops from a single trial remain within the same fold to prevent temporal leakage. Within each training fold, an additional 20\% stratified split creates a calibration set for early stopping with patience of 10 epochs and EMA decay of 0.80. We use AdamW optimizer ($\text{lr} = 10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$) with a cosine learning rate schedule preceded by 5 warmup epochs. All models are trained for a maximum of 80 epochs with batch size 128. The embedding dimension is $D = 64$. Training is performed on an NVIDIA GPU using automatic mixed precision.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Why Hierarchical Routing Works}
The 5.6\% gain from hierarchical routing can be attributed to the structural prior it imposes on the learning problem. By decomposing the 5-class problem into a length gating followed by 2- and 3-class sub-problems, we reduce the number of pairwise decision boundaries from $\binom{5}{2} = 10$ to $\binom{2}{2} + \binom{3}{2} = 4$. Critically, the most confusable pairs in our vocabulary are intra-length: ``Hello'' vs.\ ``Help me'' (both disyllabic, both starting with /h/) and ``Stop'' vs.\ ``Yes'' (both monosyllabic). The routing mechanism ensures these confusable pairs are only compared within their respective heads, never across length categories.

The gating function $P(z \mid h)$ itself achieves 78.3\% accuracy on the length prediction subtask, indicating that the length attribute is genuinely decodable from EEG. More importantly, the routing reduces within-head class diversity, allowing the dedicated heads to learn finer-grained features. This divide-and-conquer strategy mirrors how the human brain processes speech: the dorsal stream first extracts syllabic structure before phonological retrieval \cite{hickok2007cortical}.

\subsection{Angular Margin as an Information Bottleneck}
ArcFace can be understood as an information bottleneck that compresses the embedding distribution. The angular margin $m$ enforces that intra-class embeddings occupy a geodesic radius no larger than $\pi - m$ on the hypersphere, while inter-class clusters maintain at least $m$ radians of separation. For EEG, where within-class variance is high due to non-stationarity, this regularization is particularly effective. The 4.9\% gain from ArcFace (relative to cross-entropy alone) is consistent with the hypothesis that angular margins are more robust than Euclidean margins to the baseline shifts in neural activity across trials and sessions.

We observe that ArcFace provides the greatest benefit for the most confusable class pairs. When ArcFace is removed, the ``Hello'' vs.\ ``Help me'' confusion increases by 14\%; the ``Stop'' vs.\ ``Yes'' confusion increases by 11\%. This targeted improvement suggests that the angular margin preferentially separates classes that cluster near each other in the embedding space, which is precisely where phonetic overlap occurs.

\subsection{Ensemble Diversity and Voting Dynamics}
The three ensemble members exhibit complementary error patterns. Member A (kernels \{7,15,31\}) performs best on short words, Member B (\{9,19,39\}) on long words, and Member C (\{5,11,23\}) bridges the two. The pairwise Cohen's Kappa between members averages 0.42, confirming reasonable diversity. The confidence-filtering mechanism improves per-member accuracy by 1.8\% on average by discarding low-margin crops that typically correspond to inter-word transitions or attentional lapses.

\subsection{Comparison with Prior Art}
Our results are consistent with the trajectory of imagined speech decoding accuracy reported in the literature. Panachakel and Ramakrishnan \cite{panachakel2021deep} survey 15 studies on EEG-based imagined speech, noting that most achieve 30--40\% accuracy for multi-class (4--7 word) decoding. Cooney \etal \cite{cooney2019imagined} report 37.2\% on a 5-class English word task using raw EEG and CNNs. Wang \etal \cite{wang2022transformer} achieve 39.5\% with a transformer-based architecture on a 5-class vocabulary. Our improvement to 42.1\% therefore represents meaningful progress, especially considering that our evaluation uses trial-safe cross-validation which is more rigorous than random split methods used in some prior work.

\subsection{Limitations and Clinical Path}
Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the current vocabulary is restricted to 5 words; scaling to open-vocabulary decoding will require phoneme-level or subword-level architectures. Second, \method has a higher parameter count (2.1M) than EEGNet (2.9K), increasing inference latency. For real-time BCI, this necessitates model quantization or distillation. Third, our evaluation is offline; online closed-loop validation is required to assess co-adaptive human learning. Fourth, the inter-subject variance (range: 34.5--49.1\%) highlights the need for domain adaptation or subject-specific fine-tuning protocols. Finally, ethical considerations around neural privacy and biometric identification from EEG features must be addressed before clinical deployment.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
We presented \method, a deep learning framework for imagined speech decoding that combines channel self-attention, multi-scale temporal convolutions, hierarchical phonetic routing, and ArcFace angular margin loss. On the KaraOne benchmark, \method achieves 42.1\% Top-1 accuracy, establishing a new state-of-the-art for 5-class imagined speech decoding. Systematic ablations confirm that each architectural component contributes positively, and saliency-based interpretability validates biological grounding in the dorsal language stream.

Future work will pursue three directions: (\textit{i}) scaling to phoneme-level decoding for open-vocabulary communication using sequence-to-sequence architectures; (\textit{ii}) deploying quantized versions on edge hardware for real-time inference; and (\textit{iii}) validating the framework in online closed-loop BCI experiments at IIT Mandi, where human and machine co-adaptation can be studied.
